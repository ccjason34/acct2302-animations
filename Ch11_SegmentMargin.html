Segment Margin: What Belongs to the Segment?

A segment can look profitable or unprofitable depending on whether you subtract traceable fixed costs or allocate common fixed costs.

Contribution Margin
Sales $200,000 − variable costs $120,000 = $80,000 contribution margin.
Step 2
Segment Margin
Subtract traceable fixed costs: $80,000 − $50,000 = $30,000 segment margin.
Step 3
Do Not Blame Common Costs
After a $40,000 common allocation, the segment appears to lose $10,000, but those common costs do not go away if the segment is dropped.

Switch The View

Predict: if common fixed costs remain after dropping the segment, should they drive the drop decision?

Common Trap

A segment with a positive segment margin helps cover common fixed costs. Dropping it can make total company profit worse even if an allocated statement shows a loss.

Real-World Case: Ford Model e vs. Ford Pro

Ford reports its business as segments. In 2024, Ford Model e (electric vehicles) lost about $5.1 billion in EBIT, while Ford Pro (commercial vehicles and services) earned roughly +$9 billion at a 13.5% margin — the same company, two very different segment results.

A losing segment

Model e spends heavily on EV development and sells at a loss for now.

A winning segment

Ford Pro's commercial trucks and services produce strong, steady profit.

Why segments

A single company-wide average would hide both stories; segment margins make each one visible.

Segment-margin question: should Ford keep funding a money-losing segment, and how would you judge it fairly?

Before Practice

Check The Idea In Plain Language

Terms & formulas
Why This Matters

Segment margin helps decide whether a segment is truly helping cover company-wide costs.

Key Terms
  • Contribution margin: Sales minus variable costs.
  • Traceable fixed cost: Fixed cost caused by the segment.
  • Common fixed cost: Shared cost that usually remains.
  • Segment margin: Contribution margin minus traceable fixed costs.
Takeaway

Segment margin shows whether a segment covers its own traceable fixed costs.

Show The Math

Segment margin = $200,000 - $120,000 - $50,000 = $30,000. Allocated common cost can make the segment look worse without changing cash flow.

Learning Objectives

  • Calculate contribution margin for a segment.
  • Subtract traceable fixed costs to get segment margin.
  • Avoid using allocated common fixed costs to judge whether to keep a segment.
