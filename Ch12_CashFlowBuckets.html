Direct vs. Indirect: Two Roads to Operating Cash

Both methods report the same net cash provided by operating activities. The direct method lists cash receipts and cash payments; the indirect method starts with net income and explains why income is not cash.

Direct receipts$90,000
Direct payments$30,000
Net income$50,000
Operating cash$60,000

Direct method

Shows the operating cash inflows and outflows directly: cash collected from customers minus cash paid for operating costs.

Indirect method

Starts with accrual-basis net income, then adjusts for noncash expenses and changes in working-capital accounts.

Common Trap

The direct method and indirect method are two formats for the operating section. They do not change investing or financing activities, and they should end at the same operating cash number.

Check The Idea In Plain Language

Terms & formulas
Why This Matters

Students often confuse net income with operating cash. The side-by-side view shows why they can differ.

Key Terms
  • Direct method: Lists operating cash receipts and payments.
  • Indirect method: Reconciles net income to operating cash.
  • Noncash expense: Expense that lowered income but did not use cash.
  • Working capital: Current accounts such as receivables, inventory, and payables.
Takeaway

Direct explains where operating cash came from. Indirect explains why net income became operating cash.

Show The Math

Direct: $90,000 collected - $30,000 paid = $60,000 operating cash.

Indirect: $50,000 net income + $10,000 depreciation - $8,000 A/R + $5,000 inventory + $3,000 A/P = $60,000 operating cash.

Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish the direct method from the indirect method for operating cash flows.
  • Calculate operating cash flow using cash receipts and cash payments.
  • Reconcile net income to operating cash with noncash and working-capital adjustments.
