Ch 7 · Relevant Cost

The Concorde — the sunk-cost fallacy

Sunk costs are irrelevant to a decision

Britain and France kept funding the Concorde for years after it was clearly not commercially viable — over $2.8 billion spent and only 20 aircraft ever built — because abandoning it felt like wasting the money already spent. The bias is literally named the "Concorde fallacy."

Why it fits: your relevant-cost filter's whole job is to drop sunk and unavoidable costs. Debate: should money already spent affect a go / no-go decision?

Ch 2 · Job Costing / Overhead

"Forrest Gump never made a profit" — Hollywood accounting

Job costing & overhead allocation

The film grossed about $678 million on a ~$55 million budget, yet Paramount's books showed a "net loss" after a 15% studio-overhead charge and a 32%-of-gross distribution fee. In a related case a judge called the studio's net-profit accounting "unconscionable."

Why it fits: each film is a "job," and the case shows how overhead-allocation choices can flip a job from profit to loss. Debate: is the overhead allocation legitimate?

Use in: Job Cost Sheet Plantwide vs. Departmental
Sources: NYU Stern (PDF) · Wikipedia · CNN

Ch 6 · Contribution Margin

$8 movie-theater popcorn

Contribution margin & loss leaders

Theaters hand up to ~90% of opening-run ticket money to studios, so tickets barely break even — but concessions run ~85% margins and deliver around 40% of profits from only ~20% of revenue. A bag of popcorn costs a theater roughly $0.90 to make.

Why it fits: a vivid "where does the contribution margin actually come from?" case. Debate: why is the popcorn so expensive?

Use in: Contribution Margin
Sources: Stanford GSB · The Hustle

Ch 11 · Transfer Pricing

Apple's €13B Ireland tax case

Transfer pricing & the arm's-length principle

How Apple priced transactions among its own Irish subsidiaries let it pay an effective tax rate as low as 0.005% (2014). In September 2024 the EU Court of Justice ordered Ireland to recover €13 billion, turning on whether those intercompany prices were "arm's length."

Why it fits: the real-world transfer-pricing story. Debate: how should related divisions price internal transfers?

Use in: Transfer Pricing
Sources: Wikipedia · Tax Notes

Ch 11 · Residual Income

Meta Reality Labs vs. Family of Apps

The ROI trap / keep a low-ROI strategic bet?

In 2024 Reality Labs (Meta's AR/VR "metaverse" unit) lost $17.7 billion, while the Family of Apps earned about $28 billion in Q4 alone. Investors openly debate whether Meta should keep pouring money into a deeply unprofitable bet for a long-term strategic payoff.

Why it fits: a more current twin of the Xbox case — a profitable core funding a money-losing strategic division. Debate: judge Reality Labs on today's ROI, or on long-term value above the required return?

Sources: Variety · Meta 8-K (SEC)

Ch 11 · Segment Margin

Ford Model e vs. Ford Pro

Segment performance with real reported segments

In 2024 Ford Model e (electric vehicles) lost $5.1 billion in EBIT, while Ford Pro (commercial vehicles and services) earned +$9 billion at a 13.5% margin. Ford reports these as actual operating segments, so the contrast uses real numbers, not illustrative ones.

Why it fits: the cleanest real segment-margin contrast inside one company. Debate: keep investing in the loss-making EV segment?

Ch 11 · ROI / DuPont

Walmart vs. LVMH — two routes to the same return

Margin × turnover (DuPont)

Walmart earns a thin ~2.4% margin but turns assets ~2.5× a year; luxury group LVMH earns a rich ~15% margin but turns assets only ~0.7×. Different levers, similar overall return — the textbook DuPont contrast with real firms.

Why it fits: your "two levers behind ROI" animation, made concrete. Debate: which lever is each company pulling?

Ch 11 · ROI / Segment

Amazon AWS vs. Retail

Business mix reshapes overall margin / ROI

In 2024 AWS earned about $39.8 billion of operating income on ~$107.6 billion of revenue (37%+ margin) — over 60% of Amazon's total operating profit from under 20% of its revenue. A high-margin segment can dominate the whole company's return.

Why it fits: shows how one segment's margin pulls the company average. Debate: is Amazon a retailer or a cloud company?

Alphabet "Other Bets" / Waymo

Investment center & long-term option value

Ch 5 · Cost Behavior · Ch 7

Airlines selling the last empty seat cheap

Fixed costs & the marginal cost of idle capacity

Once a flight is scheduled, almost all of its cost (crew, fuel, gate, the aircraft) is fixed and already committed. The extra cost of one more passenger is tiny, so a deeply discounted standby fare still adds profit.

Why it fits: a double fit — fixed-vs-variable behavior and the special-order-with-idle-capacity decision. Debate: why discount the empty seat at all?

Ch 6 · Contribution Margin

HP printers & ink (razor-and-blades)

Where the contribution margin really lives

HP and others sell the printer at or below cost, then make the real money on high-margin ink cartridges — the same "sell the handle cheap, profit on the blades" model. The platform carries little or negative margin; the consumable carries it all.

Why it fits: the same hardware-loss logic as the Xbox model, told through a product every student owns. Debate: which product should carry the margin?

Ch 8 · NPV

Amazon — Bezos's 1997 letter

Value = present value of future cash flows

Amazon told shareholders it optimizes for long-term free cash flow per share, not quarterly accounting earnings — "shares are worth only the present value of their future cash flows, not… future earnings." A primary-source statement of the NPV idea.

Why it fits: the core of NPV in a CEO's own words. Debate: long-term cash value versus protecting this quarter's reported profit.

Use in: NPV
Sources: Amazon shareholder letter (SEC)

Ch 12 · Operating Cash Flow

Netflix — profitable but burning cash

Cash flow is not net income

Netflix's net income rose (roughly $187 million to $1.2 billion across 2016–2018) even as free cash flow sank to about −$3 billion, because content cash is paid years before it is expensed. By 2023 the timing reversed and free cash flow swung to about +$6.9 billion.

Why it fits: the cleanest "cash ≠ profit / timing differences" case. Debate: was Netflix healthy when it was profitable but cash-negative?

Use in: Indirect Method: Operating Cash
Sources: The Motley Fool · Hollywood Reporter

Ch 6 · Break-Even

Disney's John Carter $200M flop

Break-even when you keep only part of revenue

Studios keep only about half of ticket sales, so a film must gross roughly twice its budget to break even. John Carter (~$264M budget + ~$100M marketing) needed about $727 million, made ~$284 million, and led to a $200 million write-down.

Why it fits: concrete break-even arithmetic with a famous name. Debate: compute the break-even point and why it missed.

Use in: Break-Even
Sources: Christian Science Monitor · Wikipedia

Ch 11 · Residual Income · Ch 8

Intel Foundry vs. Intel Products

Asset-heavy strategic investment center

Intel Foundry lost about $2.8 billion in a single quarter (Q3 2024) on $4.3 billion of mostly internal sales, while Intel moved to run it as a separate subsidiary with its own board. Heavy assets and losses while it tries to build a long-term business.

Why it fits: should Intel keep funding a strategic, asset-heavy unit that drags ROI now? Debate: the residual-income / long-term-value question. (Note: a strategic-investment case, not a variance case.)

Keep or drop a declining legacy segment

A good business with a different return profile

GE breaks into three companies

Different divisions, different measures

GE completed its split into three independent public companies — GE HealthCare (January 2023), GE Vernova (energy, April 2024) and GE Aerospace — concluding that very different divisions deserve their own performance measures and capital.

Disney streaming vs. parks

Reaching break-even; segment contrast

Disney's streaming business (Disney+, Hulu) went from billion-dollar quarterly losses to its first profit in 2024 (combined direct-to-consumer operating income about $321 million in fiscal Q4), while the parks have long been a reliable profit engine.

Ch 10 · Favorable / Unfavorable

Boeing 737 MAX

A "favorable" cost variance isn't always good news

A cost-cutting, outsourcing-heavy culture produced short-term "favorable" cost outcomes but contributed to two crashes that killed 346 people. It powerfully shows that beating a cost target can hide a disaster.

Heads-up: the case involves fatalities — a heavy choice for an intro class. A gentler version with the same lesson: a buyer who chooses cheap material (favorable price variance) that then fails quality control (unfavorable usage / rework).
