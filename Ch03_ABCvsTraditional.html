One Big Rate vs. Many Small Buckets 🪣

A furniture maker splits $500,000 of overhead between two products. See how a single plantwide rate can hide the true cost of a complex, low-volume product — and how ABC fixes it.

Total overhead$500,000
Bookcase20,000 DLH
Cabinet5,000 DLH
ABC poolsMachining, setups, inspections

Traditional: one broad question

Use one plantwide rate: total overhead divided by total direct labor hours. Then multiply each product's labor hours by that one rate.

ABC: three activity questions

Machining$200,000 / 20,000 MH = $10 per MH
Setups$180,000 / 600 setups = $300 per setup
Inspections$120,000 / 400 inspections = $300 per inspection

⚠️ Common Trap

ABC does not change the total overhead — it's still $500,000. ABC just redistributes it more fairly using the activities each product actually demands. Traditional costing isn't bad arithmetic; it just uses one coarse driver (labor hours) that misses setups and inspections.

Check The Idea In Plain Language

Terms & formulas
Why This Matters

ABC helps managers see which activities actually consume overhead instead of spreading cost too broadly.

Key Terms
  • Activity: Work that consumes resources.
  • Cost pool: A group of overhead costs for one activity.
  • Cost driver: The measure used to assign that activity cost.
  • Traditional costing: Usually assigns overhead with one broad driver.
Takeaway

ABC is most useful when products use support activities very differently.

Show The Math

Traditional: $20 per DLH. Bookcase = $400,000; Cabinet = $100,000.

ABC rates: $10 per machine hour, $300 per setup, $300 per inspection. Bookcase = $220,000; Cabinet = $280,000.

Learning Objectives

  • Allocate overhead with one plantwide rate vs. multiple activity cost pools.
  • Compute activity rates (pool cost ÷ cost driver) and apply them.
  • Explain why traditional costing can over-cost simple products and under-cost complex ones.
